What is a DLGN, and how does it differ from an NN?

A DLGN (Deep Differentiable Logic Gate Network) replaces the computational unit of a neural network — the multiply-accumulate (MAC) — with a logic gate. Training is ordinary gradient descent, and the moment training ends the model is a pure Boolean circuit.

If the premises of a conventional NN (real-valued weights, multiplication, GPUs) aren’t yet second nature, read What is a neural network, really? first — the contrast lands much harder afterwards.

The color convention on this site

Throughout this site, orange = the real-valued world (MAC, gradients, soft values during training) and green = the logical world (bits, gates, hard values at inference). The colors in a diagram tell you where reals end and bits begin.

2. Why is it trainable at all? — continuous relaxation

Section titled “2. Why is it trainable at all? — continuous relaxation”

Choosing a gate is inherently discrete, and no gradient flows through it. A DLGN sidesteps this by using all 16 candidate gates (for N=2) a little bit at a time, simultaneously.

Each neuron holds a weight WWW per candidate gate, converts it into a probability distribution (softmax), and takes the weighted average of every candidate’s output.

  • the inputs xjx_jxj​ are also reals in [0,1][0,1][0,1] (close to 1 = high confidence the bit is set)

Substituting xj∈{0,1}x_j\in\{0,1\}xj​∈{0,1} makes exactly one term equal 1 and the rest 0 — in other words this formula is the straightforward continuous extension of an ordinary truth-table lookup. It preserves the meaning of Boolean algebra while remaining smoothly differentiable in both WWW and xxx. That’s why a plain PyTorch training loop works unmodified.

3. Two phases: soft (training) and hard (inference)

Section titled “3. Two phases: soft (training) and hard (inference)”

A DLGN model has two faces, and switching between them maps directly onto PyTorch’s train() / eval().

soft (training)hard (inference)
Valuesreals in [0,1][0,1][0,1]bits in {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}
Gatea mixture of all candidatesone gate, fixed by argmax
Differentiableyes (gradients flow)not needed (nothing is learned here)
Codemodel.train()model.eval()

The accuracy gap between soft and hard is called the discretization gap, and closing it is what DLGN quality actually means. The number to watch during training is not soft accuracy but hard accuracy — which is why this library’s training logs always report test_acc (hard) alongside test_acc_soft (→ Reading training curves).

There is also a school of thought that keeps the discretization gap at zero from the start: Hard-ST (the CAGE approach) passes a single argmax-selected gate in the forward pass while approximating the backward pass with softmax gradients. This library exposes it as selection='hard_st'.

4. Fan-in (N) as a tuning knob

Section titled “4. Fan-in (N) as a tuning knob”

difflogic — the original DLGN paper (Petersen et al., NeurIPS 2022) — fixes gates at two inputs, with 16 candidate two-input Boolean functions (AND, OR, XOR, pass-through, constants, …). This library lifts that restriction and generalizes to N-input LUTs (N = 2 / 4 / 6).

NTruth-table entries 2N2^N2NWhere it fits
24 (= 16 gates)difflogic-compatible; exact gradients, fast on CPU/GPU
416the balanced choice
664maps 1:1 onto an FPGA LUT6 cell

Fan-in isn’t a question of which is “better” — it’s a knob set by your deployment target. To squeeze FPGA area, six inputs win (the physical cell is a LUT6, so two inputs waste most of it). For training stability and CPU/GPU speed, two inputs win. See DLGN taxonomy and lineage for the details.

5. Inference is bitwise ops — straight to FPGA

Section titled “5. Inference is bitwise ops — straight to FPGA”

In hard mode the model is a Boolean circuit shaped as a directed acyclic graph, containing no real numbers at all. This isn’t “compiling an NN for an FPGA” — it means you were training a circuit all along.

This library freezes the training result into an IR (intermediate representation, JSON) and branches everything downstream from there.

The boundary from orange (reals) to green (bits) is concentrated in exactly one place: the IR. Thanks to that, you can swap out the training side and everything downstream of the IR stays unchanged, or add an HDL target without touching training (→ Architecture overview).

That the generated circuit really behaves like the original model is guaranteed by three-way equivalence.

Only when all three agree on every vector can you say it’s ready to burn (→ HDL export and equivalence checking).

6. Where DLGNs fit, and where they don’t

Section titled “6. Where DLGNs fit, and where they don’t”

Good fit

  • You want inference on an FPGA / ASIC (no multipliers, direct LUT mapping, low power)
  • Latency is tight (a combinational circuit answers in a handful of clocks)
  • The data can’t leave the device, so it must be processed locally

Poor fit / caveats

  • Reusing a large pretrained model rarely pays off. Converting existing weights into a DLGN generally loses — a native DLGN’s strength is learning Boolean logic freely, and transcribing arithmetic structure takes that freedom away (measured failure cases are in taxonomy §3)
  • Parameter efficiency per unit of accuracy can trail a dense real-valued NN. Keeping the parts that should stay real-valued (attention, for example) as they are — going hybrid — is often the practical answer
  • Training itself is real-valued computation in PyTorch, so a GPU still helps while training. It’s inference that stops needing one
