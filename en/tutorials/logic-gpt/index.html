Writing Shakespeare with a Logic Gate GPT

Write Shakespeare with a decoder-only Transformer in which attention, the FFN, and the output head are all logic gates. We train LogicGPT, which contains no real-valued matrix multiplication at all, as a character-level language model on Tiny Shakespeare, and watch it fall from the uniform baseline of 5.00 bpc down to 3.13 bpc, along with the generated text (speaker lines, word-like spellings, even line-break structure).

At the same time, this page is also the clearest example of the discretization gap. In soft mode (the continuous relaxation used during training) readable sentences come out, but in hard mode (the discrete circuit fixed by argmax) they fall apart — the harder the task, and a language model is a hard one, the more honestly this difference shows.

What you’ll learn

  1. How to build a decoder-only Transformer in which attention, the FFN, and the output head are all logic gates
  2. How to read bpc, the metric of a character-level language model (uniform baseline 5.00 → 3.13)
  3. That the discretization gap (readable in soft, broken in hard) shows up most honestly in a language model

Prerequisites

  • 1. Train on MNIST — experience telling soft and hard apart first
  • How to read training curves

▶ Open in Colab (enabled once the repository is public)

An example of training a character-level language model with LogicGPT and generating Shakespeare-style text.

In MNIST (examples/06_mnist.py) we built a “circuit that classifies images”. Here we assemble a decoder-only Transformer (GPT) from the same logic gates and train a language model that predicts the next character. In attention, the FFN, and the output head alike, every internal value lives in the logic domain [0,1] (no floating-point matrix product creates the “meaning”).

The whole of LogicGPT (with real class names and gate counts)

With the configuration used here (d_model=192, 2 layers, a 32-character vocabulary), the gates that get learned come to 2,560 gates / 48,640 parameters in total. Green is logic gates (they become a circuit as is); orange is real-valued arithmetic, kept for the sake of training, which the IR treats as a blackbox.

How attention is turned into a circuit (in beginner’s terms)

  • XNOR = an agreement detector. A single gate that outputs 1 when two wires carry the same value. It compares the query and the key one bit at a time
  • popcount = a majority count (an adder tree). Counting how many bits agreed tells you “how similar they are”. This replaces the mountain of multiplications in a real Transformer’s dot product
  • MUX = a selector. Instead of softmax’s weighted average, at eval time it passes only the contents of the most similar slot. The argmax of LogicAttention(mode='hard') is this

The rung-by-rung confirmation of why the substitution works is in The Ladder of Logification, and a comparison of four ways to look things up is in A tour of four logic-gate attention variants.

To fit in 3-4 minutes on a CPU, this sample is shrunk down to d_model=192, 2 layers, a context of 32 characters, and 1500 steps (for the serious recipe see configs/recipes/logic_gpt_shakespeare.yaml, and for experimental results see the model zoo). Even at that scale, you get as far as “word-like spellings, line breaks, and speaker lines such as ROMEO:”.

There are three things worth watching:

  1. Does bpc go down (figure 1). bpc = bits per character is “how many bits are needed to guess the next character”; picking one of 32 characters at random costs log2(32)=5.0 bpc. Everything below that is the circuit having learned the regularities of character sequences.
  2. The gap between soft and hard (figure 2). During training the gates are continuously relaxed so that they can be differentiated, but what actually gets burned onto an FPGA is the discrete circuit fixed by argmax. The performance difference between the two is the “discretization gap”, the biggest hurdle for logic NNs. In this reduced recipe the gap remains.
  3. How far back it looks (figure 3). Because this is causal self-attention, the beginning of the sequence has not a single character of context, and it should get easier to predict further along. Looking at the accuracy per position, it rises sharply over the first few characters and then plateaus — the context length this size of circuit actually uses shows up directly in that shape.

Run: uv run python examples/07_logic_gpt.py Expected result: val bpc (bits per character) drops from 5.0 to around 3.1, and generated text is printed at the end (you can see at a glance it is not a random string)

Setup: imports and the random seed

Section titled “Setup: imports and the random seed”

All we need is PyTorch, matplotlib, and this library’s LogicGPT / LogicGPTConfig. Both the training loop and the generation loop are written exactly as they would be for an ordinary GPT; the only thing swapped out is the inside of the model (matrix products -> logic gates).

The initial wiring of the gates (which two lines of the previous stage each gate looks at) and the initial truth tables are decided by random numbers. The wiring stays fixed during training too, so changing the seed makes the circuit itself a different object and changes how the bpc comes down. To get a result we can compare against, we fix the seed first.

Configuration and data: a reduced recipe and Tiny Shakespeare

Section titled “Configuration and data: a reduced recipe and Tiny Shakespeare”

Hyperparameters

Section titled “Hyperparameters”

This page runs a reduced recipe that fits in 3-4 minutes on a CPU. D_MODEL is the bit width representing one character (= the number of logic wires flowing through the circuit), and BLOCK is the context length (how many characters back to look when predicting the next one). Larger values give better text, at the cost of longer training.

REG_LAMBDA is the strength of the regularizer that pushes the embeddings toward {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1} — the knob that acts directly on the “discretization gap” we measure later.

Data: Tiny Shakespeare

Section titled “Data: Tiny Shakespeare”

The training corpus is Tiny Shakespeare (about 1.1 million characters of collected play dialogue). So that it also runs where the corpus is not checked into the repository, we fall back automatically to the embedded sample below.

The vocabulary is the set of character kinds the model handles. Here we drop uppercase, narrow the punctuation, and shrink it to 32 characters. The number of gates in the output head is proportional to the vocabulary size (vocab × head_group_factor), so shrinking the vocabulary is almost synonymous with shrinking the circuit.

We convert the characters into a sequence of IDs and split the first 90% for training and the last 10% for validation. Validating a language model means “how well can it predict a continuation it has never seen”, so cutting along the time axis is the standard practice.

Building the training data is the same as for an ordinary GPT too. The input x is 32 consecutive characters and the target y is that same run shifted one step to the right. That way one forward pass trains “the next character” at all 32 positions simultaneously, and the causal mask (below) prevents any peeking at the future.

Model: a logic-gate GPT

Section titled “Model: a logic-gate GPT”

The skeleton is an ordinary GPT (embedding -> positional code -> causal self-attention × N -> output head), and only the operators inside are wholesale replaced. Why such a substitution is possible becomes clear once each part of a real-valued Transformer is reread as “what device is it, really”.

  • inner product (similarity) -> XNOR + popcount. An inner product is nothing but a device that measures how well two vectors line up. For bit strings, passing each digit through an XNOR (a gate that outputs 1 on agreement) and counting the ones does the same job. popcount = an adder tree that counts the raised bits
  • the softmax weighted average -> a MUX (selector). A weighted average is a device for “how much of which slot’s contents to pass”. At eval time it becomes a tree of 2:1 multiplexers that pass only the most similar slot
  • Linear + activation -> LutLayer. One neuron = one truth table (LUT)

In other words attention is not “magic that weights things by relevance” but a three-stage circuit — detect agreement -> count how many -> select — and every one of those can be written with logic gates.

Training loop

Section titled “Training loop”

The internals are completely plain PyTorch, and no special learning rule is needed (that is a practical advantage of this approach). The only difference is that there are two losses: on top of the usual cross entropy we add emb_reg_loss returned by forward (a regularizer that pushes the embedding bits toward 0 or 1), scaled by REG_LAMBDA.

Why is the regularizer needed? During training the embeddings are free to take intermediate values such as 0.5, but no such value exists in the actual circuit. If the learned solution relies on intermediate values, performance collapses the moment they are fixed by argmax. Pushing them to the ends in advance is what narrows the discretization gap measured in the next chapter.

Evaluation: the discretization gap between soft and hard

Section titled “Evaluation: the discretization gap between soft and hard”

The metric used here is bpc (bits per character). It is “how many bits of information it takes to name the next character”, and lower is better. Picking at random from 32 characters costs log⁡232=5.00\log_2 32 = 5.00log2​32=5.00 bpc, and the gap from there is what has been learned. (ppl, the perplexity often seen with language models, is 2bpc2^{\text{bpc}}2bpc — the same thing expressed differently.)

And in a logic-gate NN we always measure accuracy two ways, because train() and eval() switch not “whether dropout is on” but the very form of the circuit.

We run the same 256-item validation batch through both soft and hard, and line up the bpc together with “the hit rate for the next character”. The third line of the output is the discretization gap = hard bpc − soft bpc, that is, “the part of the performance obtained in soft that could not be carried into hardware”.

Text generation: letting the circuit write the continuation

Section titled “Text generation: letting the circuit write the continuation”

bpc is a number, so we also make it emit actual text and check with our eyes. Autoregressive generation means repeating “feed the last 32 characters into the circuit to get the next character, then append it and slide the window one step to the right”. One pass through the circuit per character, and nothing else.

Temperature is the divisor that decides how bold the choice of the next character is; 1.0 is the model’s probabilities as they are. The lower it goes the more it picks only high-confidence characters (spelling gets stable, but the same words tend to repeat). We let it write from the same prompt in both soft and hard, and print the two side by side below.

Visualization: what was learned, and what discretization took away

Section titled “Visualization: what was learned, and what discretization took away”

Figure 1: training curve (val bpc)

Section titled “Figure 1: training curve (val bpc)”

What this figure shows is the total amount learned. The vertical axis is validation bpc and the dashed line is the state of having learned nothing (picking one of 32 characters with equal probability, 5.00 bpc). How far below the dashed line the curve gets is exactly the regularity of character sequences the circuit has absorbed.

Reading it: it drops sharply early on and then flattens around 3.1 bpc. The roughly 1.9 bpc it fell is the amount learned, and it flattens because it has hit the ceiling of the reduced recipe’s 1500 steps (a full recipe goes lower from here). It is also the figure that tells you whether there is room to train longer: if the curve still points downward, there is.

Figure 2: the discretization gap (soft vs hard)

Section titled “Figure 2: the discretization gap (soft vs hard)”

The figure to look at most closely on this page. There are three bars: the “learned nothing” baseline on the left, the continuously relaxed model used during training (soft) in the middle, and the circuit that can actually be burned, fixed by argmax (hard), on the right. Lower is better. The difference between middle and right — the discretization gap — is the biggest hurdle in this field.

Reading it: soft has come far down from the baseline, but hard stops well short of it. Part of the soft performance was riding on intermediate values that do not exist in a real circuit. The gap is larger for a language model than for a classification task (MNIST) because it deals with the fine differences of a next-character probability, which is why this task exposes the weakness of logic-gate NNs most honestly. To narrow it, raise REG_LAMBDA so the embeddings are pushed harder toward {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}, and train longer.

Figure 3: accuracy per position

Section titled “Figure 3: accuracy per position”

What this figure shows is how far back the context is actually being used. The horizontal axis is the position within the 32-character context and the vertical axis the next-character hit rate at that position. Causal self-attention constrains each position to see only its own left, so the left end (position 0) has no cue at all and further right should be increasingly favourable. Rising to the right means context is being used; flat means it is predicting from character frequency alone.

Reading it: soft rises sharply over the first few characters and then plateaus — the context this size of circuit can actually use is only a few characters’ worth (the bumps beyond that come from fluctuations in the validation batch). Hard, meanwhile, is nearly flat, which shows that the use of context is the first thing discretization destroys. Placing these two figures side by side reveals that the true identity of the gap in figure 2 is “losing the context”.

Figure 4: learned character distribution

Section titled “Figure 4: learned character distribution”

What this figure shows is whether the easiest part was learned. The character frequencies of the corpus (grey) and of the generated text (orange) are lined up character by character. Before it can learn the ordering, a language model first learns the character statistics of the text, so we check that it got that far.

Reading it: if the two sets of bars have roughly the same shape, the hierarchy of frequencies — space, e, t and so on — is inside the circuit. If instead only the space and e stick out, that is a sign it learned the frequencies but not the ordering, the typical failure mode when the discretization gap is large.

Figure 5: unrolling autoregressive generation in time

Section titled “Figure 5: unrolling autoregressive generation in time”

Finally we cut out how generation actually proceeds, one step at a time. The next cell records, for each step, the context, the character emitted, and the top three character probabilities at that moment.

How to read it, and what it says (the last figure on this page). Each row is one step, with time running downward. Grey is the prompt, green is characters the circuit itself emitted earlier, and orange is the one character emitted at that step. On the right are the top three characters and their probabilities.

There are two things to read. (1) The orange character enters the right end of the next row, and the window slides one cell to the right — that is autoregression itself: the circuit does not write a sentence all at once but emits one character at a time. (2) The character emitted is not always the top-ranked one — it is sampled at temperature 0.8, so the same circuit and the same prompt give different text under a different seed.
