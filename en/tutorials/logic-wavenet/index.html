Causal convolution with logic gates (WaveNet)

WaveNet’s gated activation z=tanh⁡(⋅)⊙σ(⋅)z = \tanh(\cdot) \odot \sigma(\cdot)z=tanh(⋅)⊙σ(⋅) is, over {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}, isomorphic to a bitwise AND. Using this fact we build causal convolution out of logic gates and ablate how the receptive field matters in next-token prediction of a periodic bit pattern.

With 1 layer (receptive field RF=2) the answer is not determined by the single previous bit and it plateaus at 0.817; with 3 layers (RF=8) it reaches 1.000. We further flip the input at position ppp and measure that “the outputs that change are exactly those in [p,p+RF)[p, p+RF)[p,p+RF)”, confirming that causality is preserved at the circuit level.

What you’ll learn

  1. That the gated activation tanh⁡⊙σ\tanh \odot \sigmatanh⊙σ is isomorphic to a bitwise AND over {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}
  2. How to read an ablation that grows the receptive field (RF) by stacking layers (0.817 at RF=2 → 1.000 at RF=8)
  3. How to flip a single input bit and measure that causality is preserved at the circuit level

Prerequisites

  • RNN: Logicizing LSTM / GRU — the case of holding history with recurrence
  • 5. Exporting a CNN to HDL — the basics of logic gate convolution

LogicWaveNet: an example of building “causal convolution” out of logic gates.

The recurrence (LSTM/GRU) in 09_logic_lstm.py carried state by looping along the time axis. Another standard way to handle sequences is dilated causal convolution —— the WaveNet (van den Oord et al., 2016) approach. It has no loop; each position looks only at “itself and taps exponentially far in the past”:

Each WaveNet layer uses a gated activation z = tanh(Wf·x) ⊙ σ(Wg·x). The claim of LogicWaveNet follows the same line of reasoning as 09:

So neither tanh nor sigmoid is needed. Both the filter and the gate are built with LutLayer, and the multiplication reads directly as a bitwise AND. At eval (hard) time, z is exactly {0,1}.

What we train is “next-token prediction of a periodic bit pattern”. We feed a sequence that endlessly repeats 0,0,1,1,0 with period 5, and have it predict the next bit at each position. The single previous bit alone does not determine the answer (the bit after a given 0 is sometimes 1 and sometimes 0), so whether the receptive field is large enough decides success or failure —— that is what the ablation shows.

Architecture, illustrated 1: the receptive field (the dilation 1, 2, 4 triangle)

Section titled “Architecture, illustrated 1: the receptive field (the dilation 1, 2, 4 triangle)”

The figure below draws, as a plain tree, how far back the prediction at position t looks in the 3-layer configuration (kernel_size=2). Every layer up doubles the reach, so three layers give a receptive field of 2³ = 8 steps. The 0/1 values written in the figure are eight bits cut out of the real data (the repetition of 0 0 1 1 0); the correct next bit is 1.

Note that the tree only ever grows to the left. That is what “causal” means: the output at position t never touches an input later than t (implemented with left zero-padding). With only one layer (RF=2) the tree can grab just x(t-1), x(t), and in a period-5 pattern (0,0) is followed by 1 in some places and by 0 in others, so it cannot be decided ── that is exactly what the ablation is about.

Architecture, illustrated 2: inside one layer (gated activation = bit AND)

Section titled “Architecture, illustrated 2: inside one layer (gated activation = bit AND)”

Inside each layer there is nothing but “concatenate → two paths, filter and gate → AND → residual/skip”. In this sample d_channels=32 and conv_depth=1, so filter and gate are one LutLayer each, 64 bits (= 2 taps × 32 channels) → 32 gates.

An ordinary WaveNet multiplies tanh(...) ⊙ σ(...), but on {0,1} multiplication is bit AND. It does not approximate the activation function; it coincides with it.

How it actually works (the points that matter on a first read)

  • No recurrence. There is no loop along time, so every position can be computed at once (the contrast with the LSTM in 09).
  • dilated = skip-reading. Layer l looks only d = 2^l steps back. Instead of seeing every consecutive past step it skips exponentially, so few layers reach far.
  • Weights are shared across positions. One LutLayer is reused at every time step (just like a 1-D convolution). As a circuit, one set of gates lined up along time is all you need.
  • residual is XOR, skip is OR. Even the part that corresponds to addition in a real-valued NN is replaced here by a Boolean operation that does not destroy information.
  • Only the ends are real-valued: the embedding and the final LM head stay FP (the same policy as LogicGPT). Every convolution in between is a LUT.

Run: uv run python examples/10_logic_wavenet.py Expected result: hard accuracy 1.00 with RF=8 (3 layers) / RF=2 (1 layer) plateaus at 0.8 (about 20 seconds on CPU)

Setup: imports and hyperparameters

Section titled “Setup: imports and hyperparameters”

All we need is LogicWaveNet and LogicWaveNetConfig, which fixes its configuration. The model has no recurrence, so we do not even need a helper to run a loop over time.

Hyperparameters

Section titled “Hyperparameters”

The training data is nothing more than a period-5 bit pattern repeated over and over. D_CHANNELS is the number of bits that represent each position (= the logic channel width), which corresponds to the channel count of a real-valued NN. The only thing this page varies is the number of layers; everything else is held fixed for the comparison.

Data: predicting “the next bit” on a period-5 bit stream

Section titled “Data: predicting “the next bit” on a period-5 bit stream”

The input is a 60-step sequence made by repeating 0 0 1 1 0, and the target is that same sequence shifted one step to the left. In other words, next-token prediction of “the bit one step ahead” at every position.

Why period 5? In this pattern, (0,0) is sometimes followed by 1 and sometimes by 0. So the previous single bit cannot decide the answer in principle, and how far back you can look translates directly into accuracy — exactly the right rig for measuring how the receptive field matters.

Model: dilated causal convolution

Section titled “Model: dilated causal convolution”

Three terms up front.

  • causal — the output at position ttt never looks beyond ttt. In next-token prediction a break here would let the model read the answer directly, so it is an absolute design constraint
  • dilation (the stride of the skip-reading) — layer lll looks only d=2ld = 2^ld=2l steps back. Rather than sweeping every contiguous past step it skips exponentially, so few layers reach far
  • receptive field (RF) — the length of the past that one output point actually sees. With 2 taps and dilations 1,2,4,…1, 2, 4, \dots1,2,4,…, nnn layers give RF=2n\mathrm{RF} = 2^nRF=2n

Why does the gated activation become an AND? WaveNet’s tanh⁡(⋅)⊙σ(⋅)\tanh(\cdot) \odot \sigma(\cdot)tanh(⋅)⊙σ(⋅) is a multiplication, and if the values are closed in {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1} then multiplication is a bitwise AND. It does not approximate the activation function — it coincides with it, and that is the crux of this model. The addition is handled the same way: the residual is XOR and the skip aggregation is OR, both Boolean operations that do not destroy information.

Training loop

Section titled “Training loop”

With no recurrence, every position can be computed at once, so it is enough to flatten the sequence to (T,2)(T, 2)(T,2) and take an ordinary cross entropy (there is a single batch item, but the sequence itself contains 60 training examples). We record only the hard (eval) accuracy — all that matters is that the baked circuit gives the same answer.

Running it: varying the receptive field

Section titled “Running it: varying the receptive field”

Data

Section titled “Data”

Print the first 15 steps of the input and the target, to see with your own eyes that they are “the same sequence, shifted by one”.

Receptive field ablation

Section titled “Receptive field ablation”

An ablation is a comparison in which exactly one part is removed and the change in the result is observed. Here we vary only the number of layers, 1 versus 3, giving RF=2 and RF=8, and let both solve the same task. One layer sees only “the previous bit and itself”, so the period-5 pattern cannot be decided in principle; three layers see across the period, so it is uniquely determined — the point is to check whether that prediction holds.

In the output above, RF=8 reaches 1.0000 while RF=2 stops around 0.8. What is missing is not expressive power but the width of the window — the same gate types and the same learning rule, with only the field of view separating the two results. The two asserts below pin this contrast down as an acceptance condition.

Visualization: receptive field, causality, bit-ness

Section titled “Visualization: receptive field, causality, bit-ness”

Figure 1: the result splits depending on whether the receptive field suffices

Section titled “Figure 1: the result splits depending on whether the receptive field suffices”

What to look at: the vertical axis is hard accuracy, red is 1 layer (RF=2) and green is 3 layers (RF=8); the dotted line marks full marks. The thing to watch is not “does green reach 1.0” but rather how far red stays from 1.0 no matter how long it trains.

Reading it: red flattens out around 0.8. That is roughly the fraction of the period-5 pattern in which “the previous single bit cannot tell the cases apart” — a ceiling caused by information never arriving, not by insufficient training. Green climbs to full marks and stops there. The lesson of this figure: when accuracy will not improve, suspect the receptive field before the learning rate or the layer width.

Figure 2: measuring causality (does the future really not leak?)

Section titled “Figure 2: measuring causality (does the future really not leak?)”

What to look at: we flip the input at position p=20p=20p=20 only, and color in which output positions changed. The expected picture is “pure white to the left of ppp (the future does not leak into the past), black for RF positions from ppp (the range the influence reaches)”.

Causality is a property guaranteed by the structure of the code, but the aim here is to confirm a design claim by measurement. The very same check still applies once the model is baked into a circuit.

Reading it: not a single bar stands to the left of the red vertical line (ppp) — a direct measurement that no information from the future leaked into the past. Nor is there any bar to the right of the orange dashed line (p+RFp + \mathrm{RF}p+RF): the influence stops exactly at the width of the receptive field. The two asserts below fix both of these mechanically.

Figure 3: is the gated activation z = filter AND gate really a bit?

Section titled “Figure 3: is the gated activation z = filter AND gate really a bit?”

How to read it, and what it says (the last figure on this page). The gated activation zzz of each layer is drawn as a black-and-white time × channel image, stacked from the smallest dilation down. Black is 1, white is 0.

Two things to read off. (1) There is not a single grey pixel — zzz is exactly {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}, which is the measurement behind the claim that ”tanh⁡⊙σ\tanh \odot \sigmatanh⊙σ coincides with a bitwise AND” (the assert just before checks the same thing mechanically). (2) The lower layers have coarser patterns — dilation doubles with depth, so deeper layers capture longer-period structure.
