Turning a whole diffusion model into a Boolean circuit

Turning a whole diffusion model into a Boolean circuit. Noise in the forward process is XOR, the denoiser is LUTs, and the bit reassembly in the reverse process is logic too — the entire generation pipeline closes without a single multiplication.

Even how the baseline is chosen is part of the lesson here. We originally used “denoise margin” as the main metric, but measurement showed it already reads +0.26 before any training (that is just the trivial “all pixels zero” solution). So we made the blank (all-zero) baseline explicit and re-set the passing condition to “acc@t1 beats blank = the circuit really is reading xtx_txt​”. The fact that reconstruction from pure noise still sits near blank is also written down as is, without exaggeration.

What you’ll learn

  1. Making diffusion logical: forward process = XOR, denoiser = LUTs, reverse process = bit reassembly
  2. How to set up the blank baseline that tells you whether you are beating the trivial (all-zero) solution
  3. What it means to report the metrics of a generative model “without exaggeration”

Prerequisites

  • 1. Train on MNIST
  • Reading Training Curves — how to choose a baseline

LogicBinaryDiffusion — an example of turning a whole diffusion model into a Boolean circuit.

A diffusion model is really a pair: a forward process that adds noise and a reverse process that removes it. An ordinary diffusion model (DDPM) adds Gaussian noise in the forward direction and uses a huge U-Net as the denoiser. Here we replace all three with logic operations:

Prior work — BDPM (Kinakh & Voloshynovskiy, arXiv:2501.13915) — proposed XOR noise diffusion, but its denoiser was a conventional CNN/Transformer. For a diffusion model closed over logic gates all the way into the denoiser we have found no prior work as of 2026-09 (the closest is the BDPM above). For the strength of the claim and the nearest literature see the world-first claims audit, and follow the qualified wording given there in any external communication (implementation details are in the docstring at the top of griffith_logic/models/logic_diffusion.py). No multiplication and no floating point appear anywhere; the whole generation pipeline advances on XOR and truth-table lookups alone.

Data flow (with the real class names)

Section titled “Data flow (with the real class names)”

Green is the path that stays in bits (logic), orange is the only place where real numbers appear. A real number shows up only where gate outputs are summed into a logit, and passing it through a threshold turns it straight back into a bit (the “floating point at the margins” policy).

How it actually works (in four lines)

Section titled “How it actually works (in four lines)”
  1. Adding noise = XOR. Draw a lottery with flip rate p_t = 0.5·t/T and swap 0↔1 on the bits that win. At t=T half the bits are flipped, so the image becomes a completely random bit string.
  2. How the timestep is told = a thermometer code. t is turned into a monotone bit string (“ones filling in from the left”) and stacked onto the image along the channel dimension, so a LUT can read “how corrupted are we right now” straight off its wires.
  3. The denoiser is a logic circuit. Every stage of LogicUNet is a LutConv2d; one output is a depth-2 LUT tree (4 leaves → 3 gates). It looks at only 4 bits, picked at random from the 3x3 receptive field across the input channels, and emits one bit.
  4. Removing noise = XOR ancestral sampling from the posterior. From the prediction x0_hat and the indicator of which bits are currently flipped, d_t = (x_t ≠ x0_hat), the probability of the previous indicator d_prev is computed exactly, and x_t-1 = x0_hat XOR d_prev is reassembled.

Only three formulas are involved:

  • forward: x_t = x_0 ⊕ z_t, z_t ~ Bernoulli(p_t), p_t = 0.5·t/T
  • one-step flip rate β: (1 − 2p_t) = (1 − 2p_t-1)(1 − 2β)
  • posterior: P(d_prev=1 | d_t) ∝ P(d_t | d_prev=1)·p_t-1

Words you may be meeting for the first time:

  • logit — a real-valued score meaning “the larger, the more it looks like a 1”. Passing it through a sigmoid turns it into a probability.
  • ancestral sampling — building a sample by rewinding the reverse process one stochastic step at a time.
  • thermometer code — encoding a number as a monotone string of 0/1, like the mercury in a thermometer.

At the end of this page you will find an architecture figure with real data flowing through it and a picture of the real circuit (a LUT tree) taken out of the trained denoiser. That is where you can check “which part is the logic circuit”.

The reduced recipe used in this example: The full run (experiments/train_logic_diffusion.py) trains 4 epochs on 28x28 binary MNIST and reaches acc@t1 0.886. Here we cut it down to “shrink to 8x8, T=8, 6000 training images, 1200 steps” so it fits in 3-4 minutes on CPU. What you get is the level where the shape of a digit-like blob starts to appear; it does not become a clearly readable digit. For a recipe aimed at generation quality, see the full-run script.

How to read the baselines (the most important part of this example): Reconstruction accuracy on binary images is meaningless unless compared against two trivial solutions. - copy : do nothing and output x_t as is (≈0.50 at t=T) - blank : answer all-zero pixels (= 1 − foreground ratio; ≈0.75 on this 8x8 data) A number like “accuracy 0.84” only becomes meaningful once it beats blank (0.75). In measurement it clearly beats blank (0.761) at low noise (t=1) with 0.851, while at full noise (t=T) it stays near blank at 0.754 — in other words, the circuit is certainly reading x_t, but reconstruction from pure noise can still only manage “an MNIST-like blob”. That is where we stand, and we present it without exaggeration. (Before training, acc@t1 starts at 0.761, exactly matching blank. Following this motion lets you see the moment it escapes the trivial solution.)

Run: uv run python examples/17_logic_diffusion.py Expected result (measured with seed=0, about 50 seconds on CPU): acc@t1=0.851 (blank 0.761 / copy 0.938), acc@tT=0.754 (copy 0.501), foreground ratio of generated images 0.156 (training data 0.250)

Setup: imports and the random seed

Section titled “Setup: imports and the random seed”

All we use is PyTorch and matplotlib, plus — from this library — the diffusion model itself (LogicBinaryDiffusion), the LUT convolution (LutConv2d), MNIST, and the truth-table extractor. No diffusion-specific framework is needed.

Let us pin down three words first. A diffusion model (DDPM) is a generative model built as a pair of a forward process that gradually destroys an image and a reverse process that restores the original from the destroyed one; the network in charge of restoration in the reverse process is called the denoiser. This page replaces all three of them with logic operations.

Hyperparameters

Section titled “Hyperparameters”

t / T — t is the diffusion step index and T is the total number of steps (TIMESTEPS = 8 here). t=0 is the original image and t=T is the fully destroyed state; the reverse process walks back from t=T to t=0 one step at a time.

Data: turning binary MNIST into an 8x8 bit string

Section titled “Data: turning binary MNIST into an 8x8 bit string”

x_0 — the bits of the original image. It is a training image binarized to 0/1, which on this page is 8×8 = 64 bits. From here on, whenever we write x_0 we always mean this.

Making the data binary is a necessity rather than a preference, because the forward process is XOR. An intermediate value such as 0.3 cannot be XORed — the operation is undefined unless the value is strictly 0 or 1.

There is a second reason. At 28×28 the training falls into the trivial solution. Only 13% of the pixels are foreground (white), so the denoiser can score 87% just by “answering all zeros”. Collapsing the central 24×24 to 8×8 with 3×3 average pooling raises the foreground ratio to 25% and makes the trivial solution no longer pay off — that is the crux of this reduced recipe.

The model: the three ingredients that make a diffusion model

Section titled “The model: the three ingredients that make a diffusion model”

A diffusion model is determined by the set of three — “forward process / denoiser / reverse process” — and here every one of them has been replaced by logic operations. The following are the symbols that keep coming back on this page.

  • z_t — the mask that decides which bits get flipped (a 0/1 “lottery”). z_t ~ Bernoulli(p_t), p_t = 0.5·t/T. Bernoulli is the coin-toss distribution that returns 1 with probability p and 0 otherwise; only the bits at the positions where a 1 came up are turned over
  • x_t — the image with t steps’ worth of noise on it. x_t = x_0 XOR z_t. The larger t is, the more bits have been flipped, and at t=T half of them are flipped, making it a completely random bit string
  • β (the one-step flip rate) — the probability of a flip between two neighbouring steps. It follows from (1 − 2p_t) = (1 − 2p_t-1)(1 − 2β)
  • the time bits (thermometer code) — t turned into “a monotone bit string in which ones fill in from the left” (picture the mercury in a thermometer). By stacking it onto the image along the channel dimension and letting the LUTs read it, the circuit receives “how corrupted are we right now” as wires
  • logits — a real-valued score meaning “the larger it is, the more that bit looks like a 1”. Passing it through a sigmoid turns it into a probability, and the threshold > 0 turns it back into a bit. This is the only place on this page where a real number appears
  • x0_hat (x̂_0) — the bit string of x_0 predicted by the denoiser. x0_hat = (logits > 0)
  • d_t / d_prev — the flip indicator telling you “which bits of x_t and x̂_0 currently disagree”. d_t = (x_t ≠ x0_hat). The probability of the previous indicator d_prev is computed from the posterior (the probability distribution over d_prev given the observed d_t), and the bits are reassembled as x_t-1 = x0_hat XOR d_prev. Repeating this from t=T down to t=0 is ancestral sampling (building a sample by rewinding the reverse process one stochastic step at a time)

Training and evaluation: taking the two trivial solutions as the yardstick

Section titled “Training and evaluation: taking the two trivial solutions as the yardstick”

Reconstruction accuracy on binary images is meaningless unless compared against a trivial solution. We set up two of them here.

  • the blank baseline — the trivial solution of “answer all-zero pixels”. Its value is 1 − foreground ratio (about 0.75 on this 8x8 data). On binary images this is the opponent that fools you most easily
  • the copy baseline — the trivial solution of “output x_t as is and do nothing” (about 0.50 at t=T)

A number like “accuracy 0.84” only becomes evidence that “the circuit is reading its input” once it beats blank. We measure it along hard eval — the evaluation path in which every LUT has been fixed by argmax to a single logic function, i.e. as the same pure Boolean circuit that would be burned onto an FPGA.

The training loop

Section titled “The training loop”

The training itself is ordinary PyTorch. model.loss(x0) does the whole thing at once: “draw a random t → put XOR noise on it with q_sample → have the denoiser guess x_0 → BCEWithLogits”. BCEWithLogits is a binary cross-entropy loss that takes logits directly; think of it as a loss that goes after “is each bit a 1 or a 0”, one bit at a time.

The point where this differs from an ordinary DDPM is that what gets predicted is not “the noise that was added” but the bits of x_0 themselves (x_0-prediction, following the prior work BDPM).

Here is how to read those numbers. acc@t1 clearly beats blank, while acc@tT stays near blank. In other words, at low noise the circuit really is reading the bits of x_t, but reconstruction from pure noise can still only manage “an MNIST-like blob” — that is where we stand, and we present it without exaggeration.

Visualization: the forward and reverse processes

Section titled “Visualization: the forward and reverse processes”

Figure 1: bits being destroyed by XOR

Section titled “Figure 1: bits being destroyed by XOR”

Why XOR? The bit-level operation that corresponds to Gaussian diffusion’s “add noise, subtract it later” is XOR. XOR is self-inverse: (x XOR z) XOR z = x always holds. The operation that puts noise on and the operation that takes it off are exactly the same operation, so the forward process and the reverse process can be closed with a single operation. And unlike real addition and subtraction, it never produces an intermediate value such as 0.5, so the image is destroyed as bits and comes back as bits.

What the figure below shows is precisely that way of being destroyed. Rows = 4 samples, columns = from t=0 (the original image) to t=T. As t advances the flip rate p_t = 0.5·t/T rises and more bits end up flipped.

It looks completely unlike Gaussian diffusion’s “gradual blurring”: the way XOR noise diffusion destroys an image is bits flickering and flipping. At the far right, t=T, the flip rate is 0.5 — a uniformly random bit string in which the information of the original image is gone. That is where the reverse process starts.

Figure 2: the reverse process (pure noise → generation)

Section titled “Figure 2: the reverse process (pure noise → generation)”

Now we look the other way round. Rows = samples, columns = reverse-process steps; the leftmost is Bernoulli(0.5) pure noise and the last column is the generated result. One step consists of repeating “predict x0_hat with the denoiser → draw d_prev from the posterior → reassemble with x_t-1 = x0_hat XOR d_prev” (= ancestral sampling), and the whole pipeline advances on LUT lookups and XOR alone.

The uniform noise at the left pulls itself together as it moves right, and a digit-like blob finally emerges. With the reduced recipe it does not become a clearly readable digit, but the fact that the foreground ratio of the generated images comes close to that of the training data is the numerical evidence that it has “collapsed neither to all black nor to all white”.

Visualization: the learning curves and the baselines

Figure 3: reading the learning curves together with the two trivial solutions

Section titled “Figure 3: reading the learning curves together with the two trivial solutions”

The vertical axis is x_0 reconstruction accuracy under hard eval, the horizontal axis is the training step. Green is low noise (t=1), orange is full noise (t=T), the grey dashed line is blank and the grey dotted line is copy. Plotting accuracy alone without the baselines makes the numbers unreadable, so this figure always draws all four lines together.

Green clearly overtakes the blank dashed line and keeps climbing = the circuit really is reading the bits of x_t. Orange, on the other hand, stays pinned near blank, so the fact that reconstruction from pure noise is the work that remains is visible right there. It also matters that green starts out exactly coinciding with blank at the beginning of training: the moment it departs from that line is “the moment it escaped the trivial solution”.

The architecture, illustrated, and the real circuit

Section titled “The architecture, illustrated, and the real circuit”

From here we check “which part is the logic circuit” with real data and the real circuit. First we prepare a few small helpers for drawing the figures (rounded boxes, arrows, small images).

Figure 4: pushing real data through one reverse step

Section titled “Figure 4: pushing real data through one reverse step”

The top row is the actual tensors flowing from left to right (x_0 → z_t → x_t → logits → x0_hat); the bottom row opens up the inside of the denoiser. The colours are green = the logic path that stays in bits, orange = the only place a real number appears.

The parts in the bottom row, one line each: LutConv2d is a layer that replaces the multiply-accumulate of a convolution with a LUT tree (truth-table lookup), OrPooling is the logical version of pooling that halves the resolution with a 2x2 OR, and SpatialGroupSum is the exit that adds up the 8 bits of the final stage into a logit. The gate count of a LutConv2d is “output channels × internal tree nodes”, and the same tree is reused at every spatial position (which is why 8x8 and 64x64 need the same number of gates).

A real number (orange) appears at exactly one place, the SpatialGroupSum at the right end, and passing it through the threshold > 0 turns it straight back into a bit. And the whole bottom row is what the seemingly enormous processing of the top row actually is — instead of a huge U-Net, a small circuit of a few hundred gates is reused at every pixel position.

Figure 5: the real circuit taken out of the denoiser (a LUT tree)

Section titled “Figure 5: the real circuit taken out of the denoiser (a LUT tree)”

Finally we draw the circuit actually taken out of the trained denoiser. One output of a LutConv2d is “a depth-2 LUT tree” = 3 gates and 4 leaves, and one panel in the figure below corresponds to one such tree.

Here is how to read it. The grey leaves are the input wires picked at random from the 3x3 receptive field and the input channels, and x_t (+dy,+dx) and time bit k are mixed into the same tree — that is, the circuit is matching up image bits and time bits inside a single gate. The green boxes are the names of the logic functions fixed by argmax after training (AND / XOR / NAND and so on), and the names that appear here are exactly the same names that get baked into the IR or the Verilog. So this picture is not a conceptual diagram: it is a snapshot of the burnable circuit itself.
