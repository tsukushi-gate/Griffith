Gating Is a Boolean Operation (LSTM/GRU) — Logicizing the RNN

An LSTM forget gate is exactly a 2:1 multiplexer, and the output gate is exactly a bitwise AND. On {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1} the multiplications of gating turn into logic operations — we confirm that isomorphism on a delayed copy task by comparing three models: LogicLSTM, LogicGRU, and a gateless Elman cell.

The two gated models reach hard acc 1.000, while the gateless Elman plateaus at 0.578. Under hard eval the hidden state closes strictly onto {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}, and the opening of the forget gate shows numerically, step by step, “when to keep the memory and when to throw it away”.

What you’ll learn

  1. The isomorphism forget gate = 2:1 multiplexer, output gate = bitwise AND
  2. How the difference between having gates (LSTM/GRU) and not having them (Elman) shows up on a delayed copy task
  3. How to check that the hidden state closes strictly onto {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1} under hard eval

Prerequisites

  • A seq2seq Model Built from RNNs Alone (RDDLGN) — look at a recurrent circuit without gates first

LogicLSTM/LogicGRU: an example for feeling “gating = MUX/AND” first-hand.

In the previous example (08_rddlgn_seq2seq.py), the recurrent part of RDDLGN was

which is the Elman form: at every step the whole state is rebuilt from scratch. This form has a weakness: on a pure memory task such as “keep the bit seen at step 1 until 8 steps later”, the path that carries the state is rewritten through a layer of LUTs every time, so the information does not survive.

Ordinary neural networks solved this with LSTM/GRU gating. The claim of this example is:

In other words, the gate structure of an LSTM does not become an “approximation” when lowered into a logic circuit — it becomes an exact “match”. Under eval (hard), h and c of LogicLSTMCell are strictly {0,1} —— we check that in this example too.

The task is a delayed copy: read a random bit sequence of length T=8 and, at the end, answer “the bit at step 1”. The seven bits in between are all noise, so a latch is required. As calibrated in tests/test_logic_recurrent.py, LogicLSTMCell reaches hard accuracy 100% within 200 steps, while the Elman form under the same conditions never reaches 100%. This contrast is the core of the lesson.

Architecture, illustrated 1: inside the cell (LogicLSTMCell)

Section titled “Architecture, illustrated 1: inside the cell (LogicLSTMCell)”

First we open up a single time step. The gates f/i/o and the candidate are all a stack of two LutLayers taking cat([x_t, h_(t-1)]) as input (_LutStack); in this sample’s configuration that is 9 input bits (= x 1 + h 8) → 8 gates → 8 gates. The four bit vectors that come out are combined using only two parts: a MUX and an AND.

During training f/i/o are continuous values in 0–1, so the expressions above are a weighted average; under eval (hard) they collapse to {0,1}, so the cell becomes exactly, not approximately, a circuit of a 2:1 multiplexer and bitwise ANDs. That is the claim of this sample.

Architecture, illustrated 2: unrolled in time (delayed copy, T=8)

Section titled “Architecture, illustrated 2: unrolled in time (delayed copy, T=8)”

We feed the bit sequence of the first real data point, 0 1 0 1 0 1 0 0, and ask for the 0 from step 1 at the end. The bit to answer with arrives at t=1 and never appears again, so something inside the cell has to grab it and carry it for seven steps.

How it actually works (the points that matter on a first read)

  • One gate = two LutLayer stages. No matrix product, no sigmoid. With f/i/o/candidate that is 4×16 = 64 gates per cell (in the hidden=8 configuration).
  • Gating = choosing a wire. A MUX is just the part that “lets one of two inputs through”, and it fits in a single LUT on an FPGA. The LSTM’s multiplication coincides with it exactly.
  • What the latch really is: as long as f(t)=1, c(t) does not change. As a circuit it is literally a hold circuit with feedback. Learning means learning when to close f.
  • The difference from the Elman form: the gateless recurrence h(t)=L2(L1([x,h])) rebuilds every bit at every step, so there is no dedicated path for keeping anything. It is the same figure with the MUX removed entirely.
  • The final readout: GroupSum splits the hidden bits into two groups and counts the ones in each to form logits (it has no learnable parameters). Only the counting is real-valued.

Run: uv run python examples/09_logic_lstm.py Expected result: LSTM=1.00 / GRU=1.00 / Elman does not reach 1.00 (around 0.6; roughly 20 seconds on CPU)

Setup: imports and hyperparameters

Section titled “Setup: imports and hyperparameters”

LogicLSTMCell / LogicGRUCell are the gated recurrent cells, and scan_cell is the helper that runs the loop over time. The gateless Elman type used for comparison is assembled by hand out of LutLayer.

Hyperparameters

Section titled “Hyperparameters”

T=8 is the sequence length, so the bit to be answered is carried 7 steps. HIDDEN=8 is the bit width of the hidden state — 8 lines is far more than enough to hold a single bit, which kills the excuse “it fails because it lacks capacity” in advance. If a difference appears, only the difference in structure can explain it.

Task: delayed copy (answer at the end with the bit seen first)

Section titled “Task: delayed copy (answer at the end with the bit seen first)”

Read a random bit sequence of length 8 and answer, at the end, the bit from step 1. The 7 bits in between are all noise, so the circuit has to grab the first bit and carry it 7 steps.

Why “the first”? If the task were to answer “the last thing seen”, it would be solvable by merely reflecting the most recent input, and memory would make no difference. To test retention itself, the answer has to be something that never appears again.

Model: gated versus gateless

Section titled “Model: gated versus gateless”

Building the initial state out of bits

Section titled “Building the initial state out of bits”

A recurrent circuit needs a “state at t=0”. The default of 0.5 is a neutral value standing for “logically undefined” and is convenient for training, but under hard evaluation it is a value that does not exist in a circuit. A LUT fed an intermediate value returns 0.25 or 0.75, and that propagates along the time axis, contaminating the whole hidden state with continuous values. We pin the initial state to 1.0 (= bit 1) as well, so the circuit is closed from the very beginning.

A gated cell + GroupSum classifier

Section titled “A gated cell + GroupSum classifier”

A minimal setup that merely aggregates the hidden state at the final time step into two class logits with GroupSum. GroupSum holds no trainable parameters and only “splits the bits into as many groups as there are classes and counts the ones”, so the decision itself is part of the circuit that gets baked.

The comparison: a gateless Elman type

Section titled “The comparison: a gateless Elman type”

ht=L2(L1([xt;ht−1]))h_t = L_2(L_1([x_t ; h_{t-1}]))ht​=L2​(L1​([xt​;ht−1​])) — a form that rebuilds the state from scratch at every time step (the same as RDDLGN’s K and P layers). Its weakness is that there is no path dedicated to retention, which amounts to the cell diagram at the top of this page with the MUX removed entirely. Its readout and training conditions match the gated side exactly, so if a difference appears, only the way the recurrence is built can be the cause.

Training loop

Section titled “Training loop”

Ordinary Adam + cross entropy. There is one rule of practice: what we record is always the hard (eval) accuracy. Even if soft accuracy rises, it means nothing unless the argmax-settled circuit gives the same answer — that is the most common form of self-deception in logic-gate NNs, so we look at nothing but hard from the start.

Train the three models under identical conditions

Section titled “Train the three models under identical conditions”

Seed, data, step count, learning rate and readout are all matched so that only the way the recurrence is built differs. We use “the best value reached during training” as the representative number because hard accuracy wanders between steps due to discretization, and looking only at the final value tends to understate the true ability.

In the output above the gated ones (LSTM / GRU) reach 1.0000 while the gateless one (Elman) never gets to 1.0. If anything Elman has fewer parameters, so the difference comes from structure, not capacity. The two asserts below fix that very contrast as a mechanical acceptance condition.

Visualization: what the gates are actually doing

Section titled “Visualization: what the gates are actually doing”

Figure 1: learning curves (they split depending on whether gates exist)

Section titled “Figure 1: learning curves (they split depending on whether gates exist)”

What to look at: the vertical axis is hard accuracy (measured on the discrete circuit as is), and the dotted line is the coin-toss level of 0.5. All three lines share one plot, so whether the outcome forks on the presence of gates alone can be read directly.

Reading it: LSTM / GRU jump to 1.0 in a step around step 100. They jump rather than creep because the circuit is complete the instant “which bit becomes the latch” is decided — this is not the gradual improvement of a continuous approximation. Elman, meanwhile, climbs slowly from 0.5 and hits a ceiling: with no path for retention, it is overwritten every time by the 7 noise bits in between.

Figure 2: is the hidden state really made of bits?

Section titled “Figure 2: is the hidden state really made of bits?”

What to look at (drawn in the next cell): the hidden state is laid out as a time × bit grid, with samples whose step-1 input was 0 on the left and whose input was 1 on the right. A row whose color differs between the two panels is “the bit that kept the first bit all the way to the end” — the latch the circuit found. Before that, an assert checks that the state is strictly {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}.

The output above confirms that only the two values [0.0, 1.0] occur — once the gates f,i,of, i, of,i,o fall to {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}, both the MUX and the AND become exact bit operations, so this is not an approximation.

Reading it: comparing the two panels, most rows share the same pattern while a few have their colors inverted. Those rows are the bits that took charge of the latch, and the fact that they hold a constant color from t=0 through t=7 is the direct evidence that the bit was carried 7 steps. The remaining rows are used for temporary processing of the input, so a division of labor emerges naturally among the 8 bits.

Figure 3: are the gates actually “closing”?

Section titled “Figure 3: are the gates actually “closing”?”

What to look at: the mean opening of the forget gate ftf_tft​ plotted as bars over time. When ft=1f_t=1ft​=1 the MUX selects the past side ct−1c_{t-1}ct−1​ (= keep); when ft=0f_t=0ft​=0 it is overwritten with the candidate. The bar height is “the fraction of bits that fell to the keep side at that time step”.

Reading it: the bars stick neither to 1.0 nor to 0.0 but settle at an intermediate height. That is because only some of the bits close while the rest stay open — we are averaging, so the ratio of that division of labor becomes the height itself. That the height does not collapse as time advances shows the latch never comes loose along the way.

Figure 4: the forget gate “for real”

Section titled “Figure 4: the forget gate “for real””

How to read it, and what it says (the last figure on this page). Every figure so far was about behavior, so we finish by drawing the trained forget gate as the circuit itself. On the left are the 9 input bits ([xt;ht−1][x_t ; h_{t-1}][xt​;ht−1​]), in the middle and on the right the two LutLayer stages of 8 gates each, and the text inside each box is the gate name settled by argmax (AND / XOR / NAND …).

The thing to read off is that the boxes carry concrete gate names, not LUT. This wiring and these gate names come from the same discretization path that export_ir uses to emit Verilog, which makes it the most direct evidence that “the forget gate of an LSTM” is, as it stands, a synthesizable combinational circuit.
