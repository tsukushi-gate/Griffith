Answering Pixel by Pixel with a Logic Gate U-Net

Instead of classification, we answer pixel by pixel (dense prediction) with logic gates. A U-Net that recovers the digit region mask from noisy MNIST is built from OR pooling + nearest upsampling + Concat skips, so the encoder and decoder round trip without ever breaking the bits.

It reaches a foreground IoU of 0.568 (identity baseline 0.456), and the intermediate representations at eval time stay strictly in {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}. What the interpolation and average pooling taken for granted in a real-valued U-Net get replaced with in a logic circuit is the heart of this page.

What you’ll learn

  1. How to build an answer for every pixel (dense prediction), not classification, out of logic gates
  2. Why the average pooling and interpolation of a real-valued U-Net are replaced by OR pooling and nearest
  3. Confirmation that the bits survive a Concat skip and the intermediate representations stay closed in {0,1}\{0,1\}{0,1}

Prerequisites

  • 5. Exporting a CNN to HDL — the basics of logic gate convolution
  • 1. Train on MNIST

An example of solving dense prediction on MNIST (region segmentation with denoising) using LogicUNet.

Up through 06_mnist.py the task was “one image -> one class” classification. Here we do dense prediction that produces an answer for every pixel with a U-Net whose every stage is logic gates.

The task setup:

The trick is that it is neither purely “remove the noise” nor purely “thicken the strokes” — it has to do both at once. The lazy answer of echoing the input back (i.e. the identity map) only reaches an IoU of about 0.46, so the numbers tell us whether the circuit is really looking at the neighborhood and reasoning about it.

Architecture at a glance: down the U and back up

Section titled “Architecture at a glance: down the U and back up”

The diagram below is the exact structure of this example’s settings (base_channels=16 / depth=2 / 20x20 input). The image enters from the left, first shrinking 20x20 → 10x10 → 5x5, and then coming back 10x10 → 20x20. This “go down and come back up” shape looks like the letter U, hence U-Net. The dotted lines are the skip connections (a shortcut that hands the fine shapes from before the shrinking over to the side that is coming back up).

Every green box lives in a world where nothing but 0/1 bits flow (= it can be burned onto an FPGA as is), and real numbers appear in exactly one place: the orange box at the very end, the addition performed by SpatialGroupSum.

How it works (in vocabulary for people new to NNs)

Section titled “How it works (in vocabulary for people new to NNs)”
  • Channels (ch) … how many black-and-white images of the same size are stacked. One sheet here is “a 0/1 sheet of paper on which the answer of some logic formula is written out per pixel”, so 16ch means 16 sheets. Doubling the channels at deeper stages is a trade: “we give up fineness of position and make up for it with a greater variety of kinds”.
  • LutConv2d (the green boxes) … whereas the convolution of a real-valued CNN “multiplies the neighboring pixels by weights and sums them”, this one picks two bits at a time out of the 3x3 neighborhood and learns a two-input truth table (one of 16 possibilities) to emit a single bit. The only thing it shares with a convolution is that the same wiring and the same truth table slide over the whole image. After training it can be burned into hardware as a LUT exactly as it is.
  • Pooling is OR (green, OrPooling2d) … the max pool of a real-valued CNN is “the largest of the four”, but in a 0/1 world the maximum means “1 if any one of them is 1”, which is OR itself. So OrPooling2d is the strict logical version of max pool: it halves the size while keeping bits as bits.
  • Upsampling is fixed to nearest (green, UpSample2d) … one pixel is copied into a 2x2 block to double the size again. bilinear (which mixes the surroundings) would create intermediate values like 0.5 and they would stop being bits, so it cannot be used.
  • Skip connections merge by Concat (green, where the dotted lines land) … a real-valued U-Net merges by addition, but adding bits together would give 2. So we line them up along the channel axis and show the next LutConv2d both “the sheet from before the shrinking” and “the sheet that came back”.
  • SpatialGroupSum (the orange box) … only at the very end do we need a real number expressing how strong the judgment is. The 8 bits emitted by head are counted per pixel (0-8) and the middle is subtracted to make it signed. The larger this number, the more confident the circuit is that “this is a stroke”.

As a result, every intermediate representation at eval time flows through strictly as {0,1} (we verify this below).

Getting ready: the dense prediction task and the tools for it

Section titled “Getting ready: the dense prediction task and the tools for it”

Dense prediction means, unlike classification which returns one answer per image, a task that returns an answer for every single pixel. The answer here is the 0/1 of “is this pixel part of the stroke region or not”, so the output is again a 20x20 image just like the input.

All we use is PyTorch and matplotlib, plus this library’s LogicUNet (a U-Net built purely out of logic gates) and load_mnist20x20 (MNIST shrunk to 20x20).

We fix the random seed. Which logic function each gate starts learning from, and where the noise scattered over the input lands, are both decided at random, so without fixing the seed the results would wobble from run to run.

Hyperparameters

Section titled “Hyperparameters”

BASE_CHANNELS is the logic channel width of the shallowest stage, DEPTH is the number of stages that halve the image, and NOISE is the probability of flipping an input pixel. This is a reduced recipe that fits in 1-2 minutes on CPU; the larger these and STEPS are, the higher the accuracy (the full recipe is experiments/train_logic_unet.py).

Dataset: noisy inputs and dilated masks

Section titled “Dataset: noisy inputs and dilated masks”

A binary mask is an image in which every pixel takes only 0 or 1. With binarize_threshold=0.5 MNIST is turned into a binary mask of “digit=1 / background=0”. The input of a logic gate NN must be bits, so this binarization is not preprocessing but a hard requirement. We use the 20x20 version rather than the 28x28 one because dense prediction computes pixel by pixel and is therefore heavy: halving the area makes training about twice as fast.

Three tools for making the inputs and the targets

Section titled “Three tools for making the inputs and the targets”

Data preparation splits into three small functions. materialize pulls out the binary masks, add_noise corrupts the input, and dilate builds the target.

The noise does not add to the values, it flips them. Adding Gaussian noise would create intermediate values like 0.3 and they would stop being bits, whereas flipping (addition mod 2 = XOR) corrupts the image while keeping it 0/1. The result is white dots scattered over the background and black holes punched into the strokes. Looking at a single pixel cannot tell which is noise, so the circuit is forced to use the surrounding context.

The target is not the “clean digit mask” itself but that mask thickened by one pixel with a 3x3 kernel: a dilated mask. This changes the task from “copy the input cleanly” into “look at the neighborhood and widen the region”, which stops the lazy solution of echoing the input (the identity map) from being optimal. Dilation is the OR of the 9 neighboring pixels, so for binary values max_pool2d is exactly OR.

Now that the tools are ready, we build the pair of “noisy input” and “dilated target” for the training set and the test set. The output below shows the foreground rate of the target mask (what fraction of the image is stroke region) and the fraction of pixels the noise actually corrupted.

Defining the model: a U-Net built purely out of logic gates

Section titled “Defining the model: a U-Net built purely out of logic gates”

LogicUNet has the same “go down and come back up” skeleton as a real-valued U-Net, but every part is replaced by a logic operation. Three pieces of vocabulary first.

  • Receptive field … the range on the input side that one output pixel is “looking at”. The more 3x3 convolutions are stacked while the image shrinks, the wider that range effectively becomes
  • LUT tree … unlike a real-valued CNN, LutConv2d does not multiply the pixels of the receptive field by weights and sum them. It picks bits at random out of the 3x3 receptive field, uses them as the leaves of a complete tree of N-input LUTs (lookup tables = truth tables), and takes the single output bit of the tree as the value of the output channel. The truth tables are shared across every spatial position (that is what makes it a convolution). With n_inputs=2 each gate learns one of the 16 two-input truth tables, so after training it can be burned onto an FPGA as is
  • Bottleneck … the very bottom of the U, the stage with the smallest resolution and the thickest channels. Here it is 64ch at 5x5

Why the parts of a real-valued U-Net cannot be used as they are is the biggest highlight of this example.

  • Why OR pooling … the average pooling of a real-valued U-Net creates intermediate values like 0.5 and breaks the bits. In a 0/1 world “the maximum” means “1 if any bit in the neighborhood is set”, which is OR itself. OrPooling2d is the strict logical version of max pool and can halve the image while staying closed in {0,1}
  • Why nearest upsampling … bilinear interpolation mixes the surroundings and creates intermediate values, so it cannot be used. nearest merely duplicates one pixel into a 2x2 block, so the bits survive
  • Why Concat skips … a skip connection is a shortcut that hands the fine shapes from before the shrinking over to the side that is coming back up. A real-valued U-Net merges them by addition, but adding bits together would give 1+1=2. With concatenation the bits stay bits and are merely lined up sideways along the channel axis, so the next LutConv2d can be shown both “the sheet from before the shrinking” and “the sheet that came back”

How resolution and channel width move

Section titled “How resolution and channel width move”

Let us print in numbers how the assembled circuit actually shrinks and comes back. This round trip of 20 → 10 → 5 → 10 → 20 looks like the letter U, hence U-Net. Doubling the channels at deeper stages is a trade: we give up fineness of position and make up for it with a greater variety of kinds.

Training: loss, metric, and two baselines

Section titled “Training: loss, metric, and two baselines”

Since this is dense prediction, the loss is computed per pixel rather than once per image. It is a binary choice between foreground and background, so we use BCEWithLogitsLoss (binary cross entropy that takes logits) and minimize the mean of the loss over all 20x20 = 400 pixels.

Metric: foreground IoU

Section titled “Metric: foreground IoU”

IoU (Intersection over Union) is the standard metric for dense prediction: IoU = the number of pixels where both prediction and target were foreground / the number of pixels where either one was foreground. Unlike accuracy, it stays at 0 when you answer “all background” on an image with a wide background, so it does not let a lazy solution slip through. 1.0 is a perfect match.

Two baselines

Section titled “Two baselines”

Just saying “the trained circuit beat the baseline” does not tell us what it learned, so we split the task into two components and measure up front the IoU of solving each with a fixed operation.

  • identity … emit the noisy input as is. The lower bound that neither denoises nor dilates
  • hand-written dilation … apply a 3x3 OR (the very operation used to make the target) to the noisy input. It can dilate, but it thickens the noise dots along with everything else

The latter is an extremely strong comparison point — the recipe for the ground truth handed over by a human — an almost unfair opponent that would reach IoU 1.0 without noise. The circuit of this reduced recipe only ties with it, but the way it fails is a different thing entirely (we take it apart on the right of Figure 3 later).

The training loop

Section titled “The training loop”

Training runs in soft eval and evaluation in hard eval. soft means train() mode, where each gate still behaves as a continuous mixture of the 16 logic functions (gradients flow, but it is not a circuit yet). hard means eval() mode, that is the pure Boolean circuit — exactly the one that can be burned — with every gate fixed to a single logic function by argmax.

Unlike classification, dense prediction judges every pixel, so any collapse at discretization shows up in the IoU immediately. We measure with hard eval every 200 steps and proceed while confirming that the two do not diverge.

Evaluation: the breakdown of the errors, and checking the bits are intact

Section titled “Evaluation: the breakdown of the errors, and checking the bits are intact”

Splitting the errors into two kinds

Section titled “Splitting the errors into two kinds”

IoU is a single number, so it cannot tell us where the difference between the three comes from. Splitting the errors into two kinds makes each method’s “way of failing” clear.

  • false positive (FP) … pixels answered as foreground although they are background (= over-painting the foreground)
  • false negative (FN) … pixels answered as background although they are foreground (= missing the foreground)

The expectation is that identity, which does not dilate, has many misses (FN), while hand-written dilation, which thickens even the noise dots into 3x3 blobs, over-paints (FP). If the trained circuit suppresses both at once, it has acquired a process that no fixed operation could have written.

Checking that the intermediate representations stay in {0,1}

Section titled “Checking that the intermediate representations stay in {0,1}”

The claim that “every stage is logic gates” is not just something to say out loud — it can be verified at runtime. We push the encoder forward one stage at a time in hard eval and check with assert that each stage’s output is strictly 0 or 1. OrPooling (= max), UpSample (nearest), and Concat are all designed to leave the bits intact, so passing this check is proof that “exactly the values you would get with the circuit burned onto an FPGA” are what flow through.

Visualization: looking inside the trained circuit

Section titled “Visualization: looking inside the trained circuit”

Material for Figure 1: capturing the feature maps of each stage

Section titled “Material for Figure 1: capturing the feature maps of each stage”

From here on we produce five figures. For the first one, we push one test image through the trained circuit and pull out the intermediate results of each stage. We are merely walking the forward pass stage by stage with our own hands, calling the same parts in the same order as the model itself. Each stage has several channels, so what we display is the mean over the channel axis (= the fraction of “sheets” emitting a 1 at that position).

Figure 1: an architecture figure with real data flowing through it

Section titled “Figure 1: an architecture figure with real data flowing through it”

What to look at: the contents of each stage when one test image is pushed through the trained circuit. This is a real signal, not a schematic. The top row is 20x20, the middle row 10x10, and the bottom row 5x5; it descends from the left, passes through the bottleneck, and climbs back to the right to the original resolution. The box colors are green = logic (only bits flow), orange = real numbers (from the final SpatialGroupSum onward), gray = input. The dashed horizontal arrows are the skip connections, handing the feature maps from before the shrinking straight to the Concat on the decoder side.

How to read it: the isolated noise present in the input at the far left disappears as we go through enc1 and enc2, leaving only the shape of the digit. At the bottleneck (5x5) the fine detail of the strokes is already lost and only “roughly where the character is” remains. That the 20x20 contours still come back on the right is thanks to the dashed skip connections handing over the fine shapes from before the shrinking. Only the orange logit is real-valued; every value that flowed through the green boxes was strictly 0/1 (confirmed by the assert just above).

Figure 2: the learning curve (IoU)

Section titled “Figure 2: the learning curve (IoU)”

What to look at: how the test IoU measured in hard eval (= the shape of the burnable circuit) rises with the training steps. The gray dashed line is the lower bound of “do nothing” (identity), and only once that line is overtaken has “neighborhood-based reasoning” happened.

How to read it: the initial circuit at step 0 is random, so the IoU starts from nearly zero, and within a few hundred steps it overtakes the dashed line and plateaus around 0.57. What matters is that this is the hard eval curve, not the soft one: it means what was learned by continuous relaxation survives discretization as it is.

Figure 3: baseline comparison and the breakdown of the errors

Section titled “Figure 3: baseline comparison and the breakdown of the errors”

What to look at: on the left, the IoU comparison of the three (identity / hand-written dilation / the trained circuit); on the right, a stacked bar decomposing that difference into FP (over-painting) and FN (misses). The lower the bar, the better.

How to read it: in the left bar chart the trained circuit is far above “do nothing” and nearly ties with hand-written dilation, the recipe for the ground truth handed over by a human. But the right shows that the ways of failing are exactly opposite. Identity does not dilate, so it leans toward misses (FN, purple); hand-written dilation thickens even the noise dots, so it leans toward over-painting (FP, orange). Only the trained circuit stands at a position biased toward neither, and that is the evidence that it acquired a process no fixed operation could have written.

Figure 4: input, output, and ground truth side by side

Section titled “Figure 4: input, output, and ground truth side by side”

What to look at: a qualitative figure to check with your own eyes as digits. From the top, four rows — “noisy input / circuit output / ground truth mask / remaining errors” — for six test images. The darker the fourth row (red), the better.

How to read it: success means the isolated noise scattered in the first row is gone in the second, and the strokes are painted as the same thick regions as the ground truth in the third. The errors in the fourth row tend to remain faintly along the contours — the one pixel at the dilation boundary is inherently hard to judge. Conversely, if red dots are scattered in the middle of the background, that is a sign the denoising is not working.

Architecture at a glance: the shape of the U and the skip connections

Section titled “Architecture at a glance: the shape of the U and the skip connections”

Figure 5: how resolution and channel width move (a schematic of numbers only)

Section titled “Figure 5: how resolution and channel width move (a schematic of numbers only)”

What to look at: finally, a schematic that strips the real data out of Figure 1 and leaves only the skeleton, to review the whole thing. The box height is the resolution of the feature map, the numbers inside the box are the resolution and channel width, and the dotted lines are the skip connections.

There are two things worth noticing. One is that the boxes get shorter as the image shrinks while the channel count doubles instead — exactly the standard U-Net move of “give up positional detail in exchange for seeing a wider area, and make up for it with representational width”. The other is that, because the positional information lost while shrinking never comes back, the encoder output at the same resolution is handed to the decoder along the dotted lines to restore the fine details of the contours. The one decisive difference from a real-valued U-Net is that this merge is a Concat rather than an addition (bits cannot be added).
